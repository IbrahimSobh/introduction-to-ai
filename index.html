Part A · Foundations

A short, high-level tour: what AI is, how machines learn from data, and how neural networks work.

1. What is AI? (and how machines learn)

Artificial intelligence (AI) is the field of building computer systems that do tasks we usually link to human intelligence: understanding language, recognising images, making decisions. Machine learning (ML) is the part of AI where the system learns patterns from data instead of following rules a person wrote by hand.

Think of teaching a child to recognise cats. You don't write a list of rules about whiskers and ears; you show many pictures and say "cat" or "not cat". Machine learning works the same way: show examples, and the system finds the pattern itself.

Classification and regression

Supervised learning answers two kinds of questions. Classification picks a category from a fixed list. Regression predicts a number that can take any value.

Both together: a car's front camera

Many real systems do both at once. In object detection, the front camera of a car finds every object in the frame. For each one it predicts what it is and where it is.

1Classification: what is inside the box?
The label person 94%. The model picks one category from a fixed list (car, person, bike, stop sign…) and says how sure it is.
2Regression: where is the box?
Four numbers: x, y, width and height. They are continuous values, so predicting them is regression.

For every object, the model predicts both at once: a category (classification) and four numbers (regression). The car needs both, what is ahead and where it is, to decide whether to slow down. It learns this from thousands of road images where people drew the boxes and wrote the labels, so it is supervised learning.

Where it shows up

  • Supervised: spam filters, image recognition, predicting prices or delivery times, and object detection in car cameras.
  • Unsupervised: customer segments, grouping similar news articles, spotting unusual transactions.
  • Reinforcement: game-playing AI, robotics, and part of how chat assistants are tuned to give helpful answers (lesson 4).

2. Learning from Data (models find relations)

A model is a formula with parameters: numbers that set its exact shape. Training means finding the parameter values that make the model's outputs match the examples, so it can make predictions for new cases.

An estate agent who has seen hundreds of sales can guess a new house's price from its size. They learned the relation from experience. A model does the same from data: it finds the pattern, then uses it on houses it has never seen. Because a price is a number, this is regression (lesson 1).

What is a model? Take the simplest one, a straight line: y = a·x + b. Here x is the house size and y is its price.
  • Model: the shape we assume, here a straight line.
  • Parameters: a, the slope (how much the price rises per extra m²), and b, where the line starts.
  • Training: find the values of a and b that make the line fit the data best.
  • Prediction: put a new size into the trained formula to get a price.

Predictions can be wrong

  • Bad data: errors, missing values or old data teach the model the wrong pattern. Tick "add bad data" above and train again: a few wrong prices pull the whole line off. Garbage in, garbage out.
  • Unrepresentative data: a model trained only on small flats will guess badly for mansions, and one trained on last decade's prices won't know today's market.
  • Wrong model: a model that's too simple or too complex for the pattern, as the next demo shows.

Where it shows up

  • Every ML project splits data into training and test sets before trusting a model.
  • Data quality work (cleaning, checking, updating) is often most of the effort in real AI projects.

3. Neural Networks (layers of neurons)

A neural network is a model made of layers of simple units called neurons. Each connection has a weight, a number that says how much one neuron's output matters to the next. Learning means finding good weights.

Think of a team passing a decision along. The first row reads the inputs, each next row combines what the row before reported, giving more say to colleagues it trusts more (bigger weights). The last row gives the answer. Training is the team learning whom to trust.

Same idea as lesson 2, bigger model. A neural network is still a model with parameters: its weights, plus a bias in each neuron (a small extra number, like b in the line). Two things change: it has many more parameters, and each neuron bends its result, so together they can draw curves instead of a straight line (nonlinear). Training is the same: find the parameter values that make the predictions right.

The idea in one picture

  • Input layer: the numbers you feed in (pixels, words turned into numbers, measurements).
  • Hidden layers: each neuron multiplies its inputs by weights, adds them up, and passes the result through an activation function that bends it. Stacking layers lets the network learn complex patterns.
  • Output layer: the answer, such as a probability or a predicted number.

The task above: a light should be on when exactly one of two switches is on (this is called XOR). No single straight line can separate the "on" cases from the "off" ones, so it needs the hidden layer.

How it learns: adjusting weights from examples

  1. Forward pass: run an example through the network to get a prediction.
  2. Measure the error: compare the prediction with the right answer.
  3. Backpropagation: work out how much each weight contributed to the error.
  4. Update: nudge every weight a little in the direction that reduces the error (gradient descent). Repeat over many examples, many times.
Gradient descent, intuitively: imagine walking down a hill in thick fog. You can't see the valley (the lowest error), but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet, so you take a small step downhill, then again, and again.

Training is iterative: the network goes through the examples many times, and each time it nudges every parameter a little, until the error stops falling. The small network above needs 1,500 rounds for its 13 parameters. An LLM has billions of parameters and learns from trillions of words (tokens, lesson 4), which adds up to an enormous number of simple multiplications. GPUs can do thousands of these at the same time (in parallel), which is why training large models needs many powerful GPUs, often running for weeks or months.

Modern networks work the same way, just much bigger: deep learning means many layers, and today's large language models have billions of weights (often called parameters).

Where it shows up

  • Image recognition, speech recognition and translation.
  • Recommendations on video and shopping sites.
  • Large language models (Part B): a very large network trained on huge amounts of text.

4. Generative AI and Large Language Models (LLMs)

Generative AI creates new content (text, images, audio, video, code) instead of only sorting or predicting labels. A large language model (LLM) is a generative model for text: a very large neural network trained on huge amounts of text to predict what comes next. It is the same idea as the line in lesson 2: a model whose parameters are learned from data, only with billions of them.

Think of an extremely well-read autocomplete. Your phone's keyboard suggests the next word from a few common phrases. An LLM does the same job at a vastly larger scale, having read a large share of the public internet and many books, so its "next word" can continue a story, answer a question or write working code.

Not only text

Generative AI createsExample request → result
Text"Summarise this report" → a short summary (LLMs, the focus of this course)
Code"Write a function that checks an email address" → working code
Images"A cat astronaut, in watercolour" → a picture
Audioa written text → natural-sounding speech, or a description → a short piece of music
Video"A drone shot over a beach at sunset" → a short clip

Many models are also multimodal: they can read images, audio or documents, not only create them. The rest of this course focuses on text and LLMs, because most AI tools at work are built on them.

Tokens and the context window

LLMs don't read letters or whole words; they read tokens, common chunks of text such as learn, ing or the. In English, one token is about 4 characters, or about ¾ of a word. The context window is the most tokens the model can work with at once: your prompt, the conversation so far, any documents you add, and its answer.

  • Pre-training teaches language, facts and patterns by predicting the next token across a huge amount of text. The result, a base model, can continue any text but doesn't reliably follow instructions.
  • Instruction tuning trains it further on examples of instructions paired with good responses, so it learns to act as an assistant.
  • Preference tuning (for example RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback) uses people's ratings of answers to make it more helpful, honest and safe.

The Transformer, at a high level

Next-token prediction and temperature

An LLM writes one token at a time. At each step it gives every possible next token a probability, picks one, adds it to the text, and repeats. Usually it samples (picks at random, weighted by probability), which is why the same prompt can give different answers. Temperature controls how adventurous the picks are: low temperature almost always takes the most likely token; high temperature spreads the chances out.

Think of asking two friends to finish a sentence. The careful one always picks the obvious word. The adventurous one surprises you: sometimes brilliantly, sometimes oddly. Low temperature is the careful friend; high temperature is the adventurous one.

TemperatureBehaviourUse it for
Low (about 0–0.3)consistent: picks the most likely tokensfacts, code, extracting data, classifying
Medium (about 0.5–0.8)balancedeveryday chat, explanations, emails
High (about 1 or more)varied and surprising, but can drift or make more mistakesbrainstorming, story ideas, slogans, poems

Chat apps choose the temperature for you. Developers set it when they build their own apps on top of an LLM.

Toy language model · next-token probabilities
Because the model picks likely-sounding tokens, its output is fluent, but fluent is not the same as true. That's the root of hallucinations (lesson 10).

Where it shows up

  • Chat assistants, writing and summarising, translation, and coding assistants.
  • Image and video generators work on the same "generate step by step" idea with different model types.

5. Prompt Engineering (asking well)

A prompt is everything you send the model. Prompt engineering is writing prompts that reliably get the result you need: clear, specific and structured.

Think of briefing a smart new colleague who knows a lot but nothing about your situation. "Look at this" gets you a guess. "You're our feedback analyst; label this review as positive, negative or mixed, like these two examples, and give me JSON" gets you exactly what you need. (JSON is a simple, structured text format that programs can read.)

Treat prompts like code: test them on several real inputs, change one thing at a time, and keep the versions that work. Many modern models also "think" internally before answering, but clear steps and a clear output format still help.

From prompt engineering to context engineering

A prompt is only part of what the model sees. Context engineering is deciding everything that goes into the context window for each call: the instructions, but also the right documents, the tools, memories, the conversation so far, and what to leave out or summarise so the important parts fit.

Prompt engineeringContext engineering
Focushow you write the requestwhat the model sees when it answers
Includesinstructions, role, examples, output formatall of that, plus retrieved documents (RAG), tool definitions and results, memories, chat history
Done bymostly a person, once per taskmostly the app or agent harness (lesson 7), automatically, at every step
Analogywriting a clear briefalso packing the right folder of papers to go with it, and nothing extra

The model only knows what's in its context, so a great prompt with the wrong documents still gives a wrong answer, and too much irrelevant text can bury the important part. RAG (lesson 6) and agents (lesson 7) are context engineering in action.

Where it shows up

  • Everyday chat use: better questions get better answers.
  • Apps built on LLMs: a carefully tested system prompt plus structured (often JSON) output that the app's code reads.
  • RAG and agents (next lessons) rely on context engineering: they put the right information and tools into the context.

6. RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)

RAG lets an LLM answer from your documents: first retrieve the most relevant passages, then add them to the prompt, then let the model generate an answer based on them.

Think of an open-book exam. The student (the LLM) is clever but can't know your company's handbook by heart. With RAG, a librarian quickly finds the right pages and puts them on the desk before the student writes the answer.

Why LLMs need external knowledge

  • Old knowledge: a model only knows what was in its training data, up to its training cut-off date.
  • Private knowledge: it has never seen your company's policies, contracts, emails or databases.
  • Trust: answers based on retrieved sources can show where they came from, so people can check them. This also reduces hallucinations.

Embeddings: meaning as numbers

An embedding model turns a piece of text into a list of numbers (a vector) that captures its meaning. Texts with similar meanings get vectors that point in similar directions, even if they share no words. We measure how close two vectors are, usually with cosine similarity (1 = same direction). That's why a search for "cheap flights" can find a page about "low-cost airfares", even though they share no words.

Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of numbers. Here each sentence has 3 (how much it's about animals, money and weather), so you can see the idea.

Chunking and the vector database

Documents are usually too long to embed or retrieve as one piece, so they're split into chunks (for example a few paragraphs each, often with a little overlap). Each chunk is embedded and stored in a vector database, which can quickly find the chunks closest to any question's vector.

Size matters: chunks that are too big bring in unrelated text and waste the context window; chunks that are too small lose meaning, like cutting a recipe in the middle of a step. A few paragraphs, with a little overlap, is a common starting point.

Company handbook · 6 chunks in a vector database
RAG keeps knowledge outside the model, so updating it is easy: change the documents and re-index them. No retraining needed.

Where it shows up

  • Company assistants that answer from handbooks, wikis and tickets.
  • Customer support bots grounded in product documentation, with links to sources.
  • "Chat with your PDF" tools and AI search engines that cite web pages.

7. AI Agents (LLMs that take action)

An AI agent is an LLM plus a harness: the program around the model that gives it tools (search, calculators, calendars, code, other apps) and keeps it working in a loop toward a goal. The model decides what to do; the harness does it, shows the model the result, and they repeat until the task is done.

A chatbot is like a knowledgeable friend on the phone: they can only talk. An agent is like an assistant at a desk with a computer and a phone: they can look things up, calculate, and get things done, checking each result before the next step. The LLM is the assistant's brain; the harness is everything around it: the desk, the computer, the phone, the to-do list and the office rules.

Same LLM, different harness, different agent. A coding agent, a research agent and a customer-support agent can all use the same model. Their harnesses give them different tools, instructions and rules, which is why the harness matters as much as the model.

Tools are functions the harness makes available, each with a name, a description and the inputs it needs, so the model knows when and how to use it:

Tool calling: how the model asks to use a tool

  1. The harness sends the conversation plus the list of tools to the model.
  2. If a tool would help, the model replies with a tool call: the tool's name and its arguments, usually as JSON, for example get_weather({"city": "Paris"}).
  3. The harness, not the model, runs the tool and sends back the result.
  4. The model reads the result and either calls another tool or writes the final answer.
The model never runs anything itself; it only asks. So the harness is where you put safety checks, such as asking a person to approve before a tool sends an email or makes a payment (more in lesson 10).

Reasoning, planning and the agentic loop

Before acting, the agent reasons about the goal and plans the steps ("first get the forecast, then convert the temperature"). Then it runs the loop: think → act → observe → repeat, adjusting the plan when a result is surprising, until it can answer or hits a limit set by the harness (for example a maximum number of steps).

Memory: short-term and long-term

MemoryWhere it livesExample
Short-termthe context window: the current conversation, tool calls and resultsthe forecast it just looked up

Short-term memory is limited by the context window and is gone when the session ends. Long-term memory is saved and fetched by the harness, usually just like RAG.

Where it shows up

  • Coding agents that read code, run tests and fix bugs.
  • Research agents that search, read sources and write a report.
  • Assistants that manage email, calendars and tickets, with a person approving important actions.

8. MCP and Skills (access and know-how)

MCP (Model Context Protocol) is an open standard for connecting AI applications to tools and data. Skills are packaged instructions that teach an agent how to do a task. MCP gives the agent access; Skills give it know-how.

Think of a new employee. MCP is their set of keys and logins: the code repository, the database, the calendar. Skills are the team's how-to guides: "this is how we write release notes". Keys without guides lead to messy work; guides without keys can't get anything done.

An AI app with Skills and MCP servers

MCP: a standard way to connect agents to tools and data

  • Before MCP, every AI app needed custom code for every tool. MCP is like a USB-C port for AI: build a connector once, use it in any app that supports MCP.
  • Client and server: the AI application (the host, for an agent its harness) runs an MCP client for each MCP server it connects to. A server wraps one system (GitHub, a database, a file system, a calendar) and exposes what it offers.
  • Servers mainly offer tools (actions the model can call), resources (data it can read) and prompts (ready-made templates). The app first asks "what do you offer?" (tools/list), then calls a tool when the model asks for it (tools/call). The messages use JSON.
  • MCP was introduced by Anthropic in late 2024 as an open standard and is now supported by many AI apps and tools.

Skills: packaged know-how

  • A skill is a folder with a SKILL.md file: a short name and description at the top, then step-by-step instructions. It can also include scripts, templates and examples.
  • The agent only sees each skill's short description at first. When a task matches, it loads the full instructions, so many skills can be available without filling the context window.
  • Skills capture how your team does things: formats, checklists, house style, and the right order of steps.

9. Fine-tuning (specialising a model)

Fine-tuning means training an already pre-trained model a bit more on your own examples, so it learns a specific behaviour, style or task. It changes the model's weights, unlike prompting and RAG, which only change its input.

Think of a doctor who finished medical school (pre-training) and then does a specialist residency (fine-tuning). They keep their general knowledge, but become fast and consistent at one kind of work.

Pre-trained model+ Your examples

Where it shows up

  • Classifying support tickets into a company's own categories, in a fixed format, at large volume.
  • Matching a brand's writing style, or a specialised medical or legal vocabulary.
  • Training a small, cheap model to do one task as well as a big general one. Efficient methods update only a small part of the model (for example LoRA), which keeps fine-tuning affordable.

10. Limitations and Risks (use AI wisely)

AI is powerful but not perfect. Knowing its main limitations (hallucinations, bias, privacy and prompt injection) is what lets you use it safely, and decide when a person must check the result.

Think of AI as a very fast, very confident intern. Great for drafts and research, but you wouldn't let them sign contracts or send money without someone checking.

RiskWhat it isHow to reduce it
Hallucinationsconfident, fluent answers that are false or made up (fake facts, quotes, sources)ground answers with RAG and sources, ask for citations, verify important facts
Biasmodels learn patterns from human data, including unfair ones, and can repeat themcheck results across groups, use balanced data, keep people in the loop for decisions about people
Privacy and data securitysensitive data put into prompts may be stored or seen by others, or leak through outputsfollow your organisation's rules, use approved tools, remove personal and secret data
Prompt injectioninstructions hidden in content the AI reads (web pages, emails, files) that try to take control of ittreat outside content as data, give tools only the permissions needed, require approval for risky actions

Prompt injection: a key risk for agents

An agent that reads outside content can't always tell your instructions from instructions hidden in that content. Here an agent is asked to summarise a web page that contains a hidden command. Turn the defences on and off to see what happens.

There is no perfect fix for prompt injection yet, which is why defences are layered: careful prompts, minimal tool permissions, and a human approving anything sensitive.

Human review: when not to trust AI output on its own

  • High stakes: medical, legal, financial or safety decisions, and decisions about people (hiring, grading, lending).
  • Actions that are hard to undo: sending messages, payments, deleting data, publishing.
  • Facts you can't easily check, especially numbers, quotes, names and sources.
  • When it's outside the model's knowledge: very recent events or your private data without RAG.
